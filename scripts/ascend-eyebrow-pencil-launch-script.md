## STAGE 2 — TEACHING THING ONE: THE NICHE-DOWN FRAMEWORK
**(1:00 – 3:00)**

[B-ROLL: Amazon search results page for "eyebrow pencil" — saturated. Then "men's eyebrow pencil" — much sparser.]

Here's the first thing I want you to understand, because if you get this wrong, none of the rest matters.

Most people who try to start on Amazon walk into a category that's already crushed. They search "eyebrow pencil," they see five thousand results, they see brands with a hundred thousand reviews, and they assume there's no way to compete. And in that exact framing, they're right. You can't compete with a generic eyebrow pencil. You'll get destroyed.

But that's the wrong framing.

The framework we teach is called niching down. And what it means is simple. Inside every saturated category, there are specific groups of people who aren't being served properly. Those groups are searching. Those groups are buying. They just don't have a product built specifically for them.

Eyebrow pencil? Saturated. Men's eyebrow pencil designed for men's eyebrow architecture? Wide open.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Generic = saturated. Niche-specific = wide open."]

Here's how I knew. I went into Helium 10, the tool we use for product research, and I looked at the actual search data on Amazon. Real people, real searches. Men were typing in "men's eyebrow pencil" and "men's eyebrow brush" thousands of times every month. The demand was there.

But when I looked at the results, almost every product on page one was built for women. Same shape, same color profile, same packaging. A few products had the words "for men" slapped on them, but they were the same physical product underneath.

That's the gap. There's a stigma where guys don't want to walk into Sephora and pick a women's-branded product off the shelf. They want something built for them. And no one was making it.

That's the entire game. Find the audience that's searching but not being served, build the product they actually want, and you've already won before you've spent a dollar.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Real demand. Wrong-fit supply. That's the niche."]

## STAGE 4 — TEACHING THING TWO: VALIDATION + REVERSE P&L
**(3:30 – 6:30)**

[B-ROLL: Helium 10 dashboard. Cerebro tool. Search volume numbers ticking up.]

Now here's where most people get into trouble.

They get a product idea, they get excited, they go straight to Alibaba, they order a thousand units, and then they pray. That's how most failed Amazon launches happen. It's not because Amazon is dead. It's because nobody validated the idea before they spent the money.

The framework we use is called the reverse P&L. And it sounds fancy, but it's actually pretty simple. Before I spend a dollar on manufacturing, I use Amazon's own search data to figure out exactly how much this product is going to make. I know what people are searching for. I know how much competition there is. I know roughly how many units I can sell in launch week. And from that, I can project month-one revenue, year-one revenue, and total profit. All before placing an order.

That's the difference between launching with confidence and launching with hope.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Reverse P&L = project the numbers BEFORE you spend a dollar."]

Here's exactly what I did for Ascend.

I went into Helium 10, pulled all the keyword data for "men's eyebrow," "men's eyebrow brush," "men's brow pencil," and a handful of related searches. I stacked them. The total search volume was strong. Well past the threshold I want to see for a viable launch.

Then I looked at the opportunity score. The rule we follow is that the opportunity score should be at least three times the search volume. That gives you confidence that the competition is beatable. For Ascend, the score came in over four times the volume. Better than the threshold.

Then I did the eye test. I went on Amazon, I typed in the keywords, I scrolled. Page one was full of women's-branded products. Not one of them was built around men. That confirmed everything the data was saying.

Cost so far? Forty dollars for one month of Helium 10. That's it. I knew the product was going to work before I spent a hundredth of what most people gamble on a launch.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Helium 10 (1 mo): $40 • Validation complete"]

This is the part that separates the people who succeed on Amazon from the people who lose ten thousand dollars learning a lesson the hard way. You don't need to guess. The data is there. You just have to look at it.

## STAGE 5 — BUILD-UP: BRANDING, MANUFACTURING, PACKAGING
**(6:30 – 8:30)**

[B-ROLL: Logo design files on a screen. Alibaba supplier chat. Manufacturing video clips. Product in three colorways laid out.]

Once the product is validated, the rest is execution. Let me walk you through every dollar.

The brand is Ascend. We picked the name because looksmaxxing is fundamentally about leveling up, and we wanted a name that hits that without sounding cringe. It's a name we can extend into other men's grooming products later. That matters. You're not building a product. You're building a brand.

We spent two hundred dollars on the logo and the packaging design, including the die line, which is the physical template the manufacturer needs to print and cut the box.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Design: $200"]

Now here's a decision a lot of new sellers get wrong. Samples.

A traditional sample shipment from China can add three to four weeks to your timeline. Sometimes more. For a non-ingestible product like an eyebrow pencil, I didn't need a physical sample. I had the manufacturer record a detailed video of the product, send measurements, and show me the bristle work. That replaced the sample for me.

I would not recommend that move for everyone. If you're making a supplement, a food product, or anything that goes on or in someone's body, get the physical sample. For a non-consumable cosmetic accessory like ours, the video walkthrough was enough.

[ON-SCREEN TEXT: "Sample: $0 (video walkthrough instead)"]

For manufacturing we went with a verified supplier on Alibaba with Trade Assurance and strong reviews. Eyebrow pencils are a category these factories have been making for decades. The build quality is reliable.

But here's the wrinkle that's specific to this category. Color.

When we sell a flavor-based product, you cover the obvious flavors and you stop. Vanilla, chocolate, strawberry covers most of the demand.

Eyebrows are different. With color, the customer has to match their hair, or the product is unusable. So I couldn't launch one color. I had to launch a color line.

## VOICE / DELIVERY NOTES (for whoever records this)

1. **Travis cadence:** short sentences. Pause. Then the next thought. Don't rush.
2. **Anti-guru:** don't oversell. Real numbers, real costs, real mistakes. The credibility comes from the honesty.
3. **Specific over abstract:** "two hundred dollars on the logo" beats "we invested in branding."
4. **Names land harder than adjectives:** AJ, Brent, Wyatt, Kalina, Hank — say the names.
5. **Pain-value rhythm:** every value block returns to the pain ("that's where most people fail") before pivoting to the solution.
6. **WIIFM filter:** every sentence should answer "what's in it for the viewer." If it's just about us, cut it.
7. **No em-dashes.** Use periods or commas. Em-dashes break the spoken rhythm.
8. **High energy on the open and the close.** Calmer in the middle for the teaching moments.
9. **Don't pre-apologize or hedge.** "I think" / "kind of" / "sort of" — kill those.
10. **Direct eye contact with camera** during all CTA moments. Walk the viewer through it like you're sitting across from them.
